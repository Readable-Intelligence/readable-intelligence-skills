# Part 05 — The proof sketch in the introduction

The proof sketch in the introduction explains how the main results are proved. Its reader is a specialist in the paper's field who has read the introduction up to that point, but not the body. From it they should understand what each main proof turns on: its decisive ideas, not every step it needs. For each decisive idea, they should understand why the proof needs it, how it works, and where in the body it is carried out. Where a proof follows a known method, naming the method is enough for what the proof shares with it. The other steps, and how each idea is carried out, are the body's business; the reader should read nothing they do not need.

## Acceptance — all required

- A specialist who has read the introduction up to that point, but not the body, can find in the proof sketch what each main proof turns on: its decisive ideas, and for each, why the proof needs it, how it works, and where in the body it is carried out.
- The proof sketch gives that reader nothing they do not need for this. It does not explain every step, or how each idea is carried out; where a proof follows a known method, it names the method for what the proof shares with it.
- Every sentence of the proof sketch is true of the paper as written. Each reason is one the body gives or that follows directly from it, or one a cited source states.
- The actual statements, proofs, abstract and novelty claims agree. No dependence on an external result is silently converted into a result proved here.
