# Examples for 02-statements

## Abstract examples

These examples illustrate decisions about information and reading effort; they are neither replacement templates nor a complete list of manifestations.

A theorem introduces a complete metric space `Z` and an isometry from `M` onto a dense subspace of `Z`. A later result may use that particular map to compare extensions, making the witness and map useful output. If only existence matters, compare named and unnamed formulations in their actual context. Neither shortening nor changing the subject settles which one is clearer. The assertion does not imply that `M` itself is complete.

Replacing “there exists B related to A, with property P” by “A admits an object with property P” can leave the same detour while hiding its name or relation. Determine whether the result supplies B and its specified data, establishes a conclusion about A through B, or needs a shared convention stated separately. In the first case the witness may belong in the statement; in the second its construction may belong in the proof. Preserve the exact relation: P(B) need not imply P(A). A specified comparison map, naturality in a parameter, or compatible choices can make the auxiliary data essential. The issue is the reader's unnecessary translation between assertions, not an occurrence of “admits” or “there exists.”

In a bound `error ≤ C(d) ε`, dependence on dimension is substantive information. Removing the symbol `C` by writing `O(ε)` is not a simplification unless the same uniformity is explicit.

## Hypothesis notation versus a proof device

“Let (K_i, u_ij) be an inverse system satisfying ...” may need its names to specify the transition maps and their compatibility, even if the conclusion concerns only a limit object. Replacing every component name by “the corresponding component” can make the statement worse. This differs from introducing a named resolution merely to state that an already specified object has a resolved model. Also, writing an intrinsic output as the value of a newly named adjoint does not justify making the adjoint construction the theorem's wrapper; put that construction in the proof unless the adjoint itself is useful output. Moving a named resolution from a theorem into the paragraph immediately before it leaves the same burden on the reader. If the result and its applications can be read without that resolution, keep its definition inside the proof; a sentence explaining the result’s purpose does not need the resolution’s name. Removing the name from “there is an auxiliary approximating sequence, and f has property P” can still leave proof execution in the statement. If the sequence serves only to prove P, keep its existence and construction in the proof. If its specified error bounds are independently useful output, retain those data and explain their role.

## Moving a definition preserves its domain

A proof defines an n-by-n matrix for sufficiently large positive integers n. Moving that definition before the statement with only the bound n > -3 silently admits negative matrix sizes. Retain the positive-integer domain and the actual size restrictions. After removing a named decomposition from a statement, also check every coefficient indexed by that decomposition in the proof: the decomposition may move, but its used coefficients cannot become undefined. Likewise, a definition of an operator A_t does not define A_{t,n}. If a later formula uses A_{t,n}, declare its dependence on n and any normalization, or use the already declared object consistently. A reader should not have to infer that it is an alias for B_{t,n}/n.

Matrix inversion is defined for every invertible real square matrix, but the identity A^(-1)=A^T holds only for orthogonal matrices. Declaring A to be invertible closes the notation and still leaves the asserted identity false. Check the hypothesis of the law separately from the existence of its terms.

If a standing convention lets the coefficient field be arbitrary, a lemma proved under that convention is not restricted to characteristic zero merely because all later applications use that case. Making its field explicit must preserve the original scope; checking only those applications would miss the lost cases.

## A fixed structure is not an unspecified available one

“The given metric d is complete” differs from “the underlying space admits a complete metric.” A proof using completeness of d needs that hypothesis about d, even if the space has another complete metric. Eliminating a disposable name must not erase the identification between the structure in the hypotheses and the one the conclusion or proof actually uses. Express the same logical contract economically. For a nested example, suppose the original already identifies the specified bundle E → B with a model bundle E′ → B′ over a specified identification of bases. Merely stating an isomorphism of total spaces, even an equivariant one, does not identify those projections or the given base B. State the bundle identification over the specified base map when that is the original assumed relationship. The construction and verification of a model are proof material; naming its existential output in a statement is a separate clarity choice.

Suppose the original standing convention says every displayed identification of its inner-product spaces is isometric, and a theorem expressly uses that convention. Making “isometric” explicit before the rewritten theorem restores the existing contract; do not discard the convention, introduce an unrelated second metric and manufacture a missing-isometry problem. If the original instead specifies only a linear isomorphism and nowhere incorporates an isometry, the proof’s need for one cannot add that hypothesis silently. Recover the whole original contract first, then make its necessary bindings readable at the statement.

## A useful model is not a disposable witness

A statement about an object through an equivalent complex may retain an avoidable intermediate assertion even after the complex is unnamed. Ask what the equivalence and the complex actually supply. A lemma computing cohomology by a concrete finite complex with specified differential supplies usable computational information. Naming that complex and its differential can make the formula substantially clearer; another proof’s computation with them is a real use. Do not demote this output solely because its formula could be spelled out in a long sentence. Still move an auxiliary resolution used only to establish that computation into the proof.

Suppose a lemma computes a previously specified quantity f(p), but its setup first names g(p)=f(p)/c(p), and the conclusion says g(p)=I(p), where c(p) is fixed and nonzero. Compare the complete alternative f(p)=c(p)I(p). The useful integral or computational formula I(p) may keep its notation; that does not by itself justify g. If g has no separate role and the direct formula is as clear, retain g only inside the proof if needed. If g instead has independently used structure or the direct formula obscures the calculation, explain that actual benefit. An entry declaring g in a notation inventory verifies its binding, not its necessity; connect that entry to g’s own complete-formulation comparison. This is a per-name decision, not a ban on normalization or intermediate quantities.
